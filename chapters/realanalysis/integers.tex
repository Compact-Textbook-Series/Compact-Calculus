\chapter{The integers}

\begin{notation}{The integers}{}
  The integers will be marked with a dagger. E.g., \(2\) is a natural number, but \(\Zint{2}\) is an
  integer.
\end{notation}

\section{Invitation: an informal introduction to the integers}

In this section, I shall not refrain myself from using the subtraction sign, as it better
illustrates what we're going to be doing in this chapter.

Defining the integers is like defining subtraction and negatives. In a sense, the integers is an
extension of the naturals, because as you would see, the system of the naturals are already
sufficient to define the integers.

One can define an integer to be a difference between two naturals:
\begin{equation}
  \Zint{a} = n - m.
\end{equation}
The integer \(\Zint{a}\) is negative if \(n < m\), positive if \(n > m\), and zero if \(n = m\).
This means, the integer is just an ordered pair $(n, m)$, which formally represents \(n - m\). But
notice that this definition alone wouldn't suffice, as there are many pairs of naturals that can
represent one integer. E.g., $(5, 3)$ and $(4, 2)$, and \((10, 8)\) are all different pairs of
naturals, but they all represent the integer $\Zint{2}$. So, we need some way to tell whether two
pair of naturals represent the same integer or not.

Consider two integers $(n, m)$ and $(k, l)$. These two integers are equivalent
\ifft
\begin{equation}
  n - m = k - l.
\end{equation}
From the \emph{a posteriori} knowledge of subtraction, we can move the terms around to get
\begin{equation}
  n + l = k + m.
\end{equation}
Thus, we could say that two ordered pairs of natural numbers \((n, m)\) and \((k, l)\) represents
the same integer \ifft \(n + l = k + m\). In other words, they are equivalent when interpretted as
integers. This equivalence relation is denoted as
\begin{equation}
  (n, m) \sim (k + m).
\end{equation}

Addition and multiplication of integers can also be defined via the \emph{a posteriori} knowledge of
subtraction. If $\Zint{a} = (n, m)$ and $\Zint{b} = (k, l)$, then
\begin{align}
  \Zint{a} + \Zint{b} & = (n, m) + (k, l)   \\
                      & = (n - m) + (k - l) \\
                      & = (n + k) - (m + l) \\
                      & = (n + k, m + l),
\end{align}
and
\begin{align}
  \Zint{a}\Zint{b} & = (n, m) \times (k, l)  \\
                   & = (n - m)(k - l)        \\
                   & = nk - nl - mk + ml     \\
                   & = (nk + ml) - (nl + mk) \\
                   & = (nk + ml, nl + mk).
\end{align}
Negative integers can also be defined by reversing the pair. If $(n, m) = n - m = \Zint{a}$, then
$(m, n) = m - n = -\Zint{a}$. Then, subtraction is simply defined by
$\Zint{a} - \Zint{b} = \Zint{a} + (-\Zint{b})$

Do notice that this method of construction has a flaw: it's not as rigorous as it should be. The
ordered pair \((n, m)\) isn't actually \emph{equal} to an integer. But rather, it is the collection
of all ordered pairs that are related by the equivalence relation defined earlier that represents an
integer. A collection of this kind is called an \textbf{equivalence class}, which is treated in more
detail in the next section.

\section{Relations, equivalence, equivalence classes}

I'd like to point out that the method of constructing equivalence classes are pretty common in
mathematics. E.g., the rationals are also constructed this way. Informally, a rational number is a
number that can be represented by fractions. And in some sense, a fraction is just a pair of
integers. Two rationals \((\Zint{a}, \Zint{b})\) and \((\Zint{c}, \Zint{d})\) are equivalent \ifft
\begin{equation}
  \Zint{a} \Zint{d} = \Zint{b} \Zint{c},
\end{equation}
or informally, \(\frac{\Zint{a}}{\Zint{b}} = \frac{\Zint{c}}{\Zint{d}}\). A rational is
then the equivalence classes of all pairs of integers that satisfy the equation
above. The reals and the complex numbers are also constructed using equivalence
classes, although a much more intricate ones. So, I feel like an entire section
devoted to relations, equivalence, and equivalence classes are well justified. Hence,
we start with relations itself.

\begin{df}{Relations}{}
  A relation \(\mathcal{R}\) between two sets \(A\) and \(B\) is a subset of \(A \times B\). If
  \(a\) relates to \(b\) by the relation \(\mathcal{R}\), then \((a, b) \in \mathcal{R}\), and we
  write
  \begin{equation}
    a \mathrel{\mathcal{R}} b.
  \end{equation}
\end{df}

Such examples of relations are the equal sign (\(=\)). On the naturals,
\begin{equation}
  \mathord{=_{\mathbb{N}}} = \{(n, n) : n \in \mathbb{N}\}
\end{equation}
where we've used a subscript \(\mathbb{N}\) to denote that this equality sign is a relation on the
naturals. So, we can say that
\begin{align}
  & 3 = 3 ~ \textrm{is true},     & \textrm{because} \quad (3, 3) & \in \mathord{=_{\mathbb{N}}},   \\
  & 2 = 4 ~ \textrm{is not true}, & \textrm{because} \quad (2, 4) & \notin \mathord{=_{\mathbb{N}}}
\end{align}
Another such relation is the less than/more than symbol. Here, we mention only the less than symbol.
\begin{equation}
  \mathord{<_{\mathbb{N}}} = \{(n, m) : n, m \in \mathbb{N} \land \exists (k \in \mathbb{N}) : n + k = m\}.
\end{equation}
Hence,
\begin{align}
  & 3 < 10 ~ \textrm{is true}, & \textrm{because} \quad 3 + 7 & = 10 & \textrm{hence} \quad (3, 10) & \in \mathord{<_{\mathbb{N}}}.
\end{align}
But, \(4 < 1\) is not true because there are no such naturals which when added to \(4\) yields
\(1\).

Note that the statement \(a \mathrel{\mathcal{R}} b\) is itself a proposition. It's either true or
false. But most of the time, we only care about two related things. Hence, when
\(a \mathrel{\mathcal{R}} b\) is written, it automatically means that the proposition
\(a \mathrel{\mathcal{R}} b\) is true and thus \((a, b) \in \mathcal{R}\).

To say that two things are \emph{not} related, we use the following definition:
\begin{df}{Complements of a relation}{}
  Let \(\mathcal{R}\) be a relation from \(A\) to \(B\). Define \(\mathcal{R}^\complement\) as the
  relation \((A \times B) \smin \mathcal{R}\).
\end{df}

Usually, the complement of a relation shows up in the \enquote{not} form rather than the complement
form. E.g., not equal to (\(\neq\)), not a subset of (\(\nsubseteq\)), not greater than or equal to
(\(\ngeq\)), etc. This is due to the following proposition:

\begin{prop}{Negation of a relation, and its complement}{}
  Let \(\mathcal{R}\) be a relation between set \(A\) and \(B\). Then, \(a \mathrel{\mathcal{R}} b\)
  \ifft \(\neg (a \mathrel{\mathcal{R}^{\complement}} b)\).
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
  If \(a \mathrel{\mathcal{R}} b\) then \((a, b) \in \mathcal{R} \subseteq A \times B\). Hence,
  \((a, b) \notin (A \times B) \smin \mathcal{R} = \mathcal{R}^{\complement}\). The converse then be
  easily justified by working the proof backwards.
\end{proof}

In mathematics, we're often interested in relations about equivalences. We define equivalence
relations as follows:

\begin{df}{Equivalence relation}{}
  A relation \(\mathcal{E}\) is an equivalence relation \ifft it is defined on a single set \(A\),
  and satisfies the following properties:
  \begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item Reflexivity: \(\forall (a \in A) : a \mathrel{\mathcal{E}} a\)
    \item Symmetric:
          \(\forall (a, b \in A): (a \mathrel{\mathcal{E}} b) \implies (b \mathrel{\mathcal{E}} a)\)
    \item Transitive:
          \(\forall(a, b, c \in A): \ab\big[(a \mathrel{\mathcal{E}} b) \land (b \mathrel{\mathcal{E}} c)] \implies (a \mathrel{\mathcal{R}} c)\)
  \end{enumerate}
\end{df}

Here are some examples on of equivalence relations:

\begin{exmp}{Equivalence relations}{}
  \begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{The equality sign on the naturals}
    \item \textbf{Parallel lines on the Euclidean plane}
    \item \textbf{Congruence of triangles}
    \item \textbf{Modulo arithmetic}
    \item \textbf{Equivalence of integers} (proven later)
  \end{itemize}

  Note that we define equivalences of modulo arithmetic to be \(n \equiv_k m\)
  \ifft
  \begin{equation}
    \exists(a \in \mathbb{N}) : (n + ak = m) \lor (m + ak = n).
  \end{equation}
  Note that we've opted for the logical or because we haven't defined subtraction yet. In standard
  notation, this equivalence relation is written as \(n \equiv m \mod k\).

  It is left as a simple exercise to convice yourself that the first four relations are obeys the
  rules of equivalence relations.
\end{exmp}

As you can probably see, there are a lot of equivalence relations in mathematics. Non-equivalence
relations are quite rare. Still, it is still good to look at some of them to form a better picture.

\begin{exmp}{Non-equivalence relations}{}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The less than or equal to relation on the naturals} is reflexive and transitive,
          but not symmetric: \(3 < 4\) doesn't imply \(4 < 3\).
    \item \textbf{The less than relation on the naturals} is transitive, but not symmetric and
          reflexive, as \(3 < 3\) is false.
    \item \textbf{The improper subset} (if interpretted as a relation)\footnote{A relation must be
          defined as a cartesian product of sets. Without a universe, the subset relation must be
          defined on the set of all sets, which doesn't exist in standard set theory. Hence, a
          universe must be given.} is reflexive, but not symmetric, as \(A \subseteq B\) doesn't
          imply \(B \subseteq A\). It's also not transitive, as it's possible for \(A \subseteq B\)
          and \(B \subseteq C\), but \(A \nsubseteq C\) because there are some elements that are in
          \(A\) but is neither in \(B\) or \(C\).
    \item \textbf{The \enquote{is a child of} relation.} Since I'm not my own child, the relation
          isn't reflexive. A person \(A\) is my child shouldn't, \emph{in the assumption that no
          incest has happened}, implies that I'm person \(A\)'s child; hence, the relation isn't
          symmetric. Person \(A\) being person \(B\)'s child and person \(C\) being person \(B\)'s
          child doesn't, \emph{in the assumption that no incest has happened}, implies that person
          \(C\) is person \(A\)'s child; hence, the relation is not transitive.
  \end{itemize}
\end{exmp}

\paragraph{Equal vs equivalent}

Equivalent and equal are \textbf{not} the same. Two objects are \emph{equivalent} when they share
some of the same property that's conveyed via the equivalence relations. Two things are \emph{equal}
when they are \emph{literally the same thing}. E.g., under the parallel lines on the Euclidean plane
equivalence relations, we can have two lines that are equivalent to each other, i.e., parallel to
each other, but they aren't the same line.

We then define an equivalence class as follows:
\begin{df}{Equivalence classes}{}
  Given an equivalence relation \(\mathcal{E}\) on a set \(A\), an equivalence class of \(a \in A\),
  denoted \([a]\), is the set of all elements of \(b \in A\) such that
  \(a \mathrel{\mathcal{E}} b\).
\end{df}

E.g., in the naturals modulo three,
\begin{align}
  [1] & = \{1, 4, 7, 10, 13, \dots\}, \\
  [2] & = \{2, 5, 8, 11, 14, \dots\}, \\
  [3] & = \{0, 3, 6, 9, 12, \dots\}
\end{align}
Each equivalence classes can be thought like a bin that collects all the objects that are equivalent
to each other. E.g., in the class \([1]\) formed by the equivalence relation \(\equiv_3\), we get
\(1 \equiv_3 4\), \(7 \equiv_3 13\), \(10 \equiv_3 4\), etc.

One useful properties of equivalence classes formed by \emph{any} equivalence relation on \emph{any}
set is:
\begin{prop}{Equivalence classes are disjoint}{integers-equivalence_class_disjoint}
  Let \(\mathcal{E}\) be an equivalence relation on a set \(A\). Then,
  \begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item If \(\exists(x \in X) \exists(y \in Y) : x \mathrel{\mathcal{E}^{\complement}} y\), then
          \([X] \cap [Y] = \emptyset\).
    \item If \(\exists(x \in X) \exists(y \in Y) : x \mathrel{\mathcal{E}} y\), then \([X] = [Y]\).
  \end{itemize}
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
  For the first part, assume that \(\exists (a \in A) : a \in [X] \land a \in [Y]\). Then,
  \(a \mathrel{\mathcal{E}} x\) and \(a \mathrel{\mathcal{E}} y\). By symmetry,
  \(x \mathrel{\mathcal{E}} a\) and by transitivity, \(x \mathrel{\mathcal{E}} y\), which
  contradicts the assumption that \(x \mathrel{\mathcal{E}^{\complement}} y\). Hence, \(a\) cannot
  exist --- there is no elements that are in both \([X]\) and \([Y]\).

  For the second part, assume that \(a \in [X]\), then \(a \mathrel{\mathcal{E}} x\). By
  transitivity, \(a \mathrel{\mathcal{E}} y\) as well, hence \(a \in [Y]\). Swapping the roles of
  \([X]\) and \([Y]\) yields that if \(a \in [Y]\), \(a \in [X]\). We have a double inclusion, and
  \([X] = [Y]\).
\end{proof}

Another way to phrase the preceding proposition is that two equivalence classes are either
identically the same or are completely disjoint.

This is the reason that equivalence classes is a viable way to unambiguously define objects new
mathematical objects out of old: there is no overlap. The integers are then defined as follows:

\begin{df}{Integers}{integers_definition}
  Define an equivalence relation \(\mathord{\sim} \subseteq \mathbb{N} \times \mathbb{N}\) as
  \begin{equation}
    (n, m) \sim (k, l) \quad \textrm{\ifft} \quad n + l = m + k.
  \end{equation}
  An integer is then an equivalence class formed by the equivalence relation \(\sim\). The set of
  all integers is then denoted \(\mathbb{Z}\).

  Every ordered pair of naturals is then an integer in this perspective.
\end{df}

Note that this relation here is \emph{not} equality in the sense that \((n, m) \sim (k, l)\) doesn't
imply that \(n = k\) and \(m = l\) by equality of ordered pairs. In order to establish the sense of
equality, we need something stronger: the substitution axiom under operations, which will be
discussed in the next section.

We shall adopt the notation chosen earlier:
\begin{notation}{Integers}{}
  Denote the equivalence class \([a]\) of integers by \(\Zint{a}\).
\end{notation}
For now, \(a\) doesn't have any meaning. It doesn't correspond to any resemblance of the natural
numbers. It is just a placeholder symbol for some equivalence classes. But I shall still give you an
informal concept for now:

We use the symbol \(a\) of the naturals to denote all the equivalence class \([(a, 0)]\). So, the
integer \(\Zint{3}\) is the equivalence class \([(3, 0)]\) which is the set
\begin{equation}
  \{(3, 0), (4, 1), (5, 2), (6, 3), (7, 4), \dots\}
\end{equation}
The integer \(\Zint{0}\) is then the equivalence class \([(0, 0)]\) which is the set
\begin{equation}
  \{(0, 0), (1, 1), (2, 2), (3, 3), (4, 4), \dots\}
\end{equation}
The \emph{negative integers} is the equivalence class of the form \([(0, a)]\), which is denoted
\(\Zint{-a}\). E.g., \(\Zint{-6}\) is the equivalence class \([(0, 6)]\) which is the set
\begin{equation}
  \{(0, 6), (1, 7), (2, 8), (3, 9), (4, 10), \dots\}.
\end{equation}
However, notice that this \emph{needs} trichotomy. We must prove that the integers fall in only
three boxes: positive, zero, or negative, and no more. This problem is delegated for later in the
chapter.

Before we do anything, let's first justify that the relation defined in the definition of integers
is actually an equivalence relation.

\begin{prop}{The defining relations of the integer is an equivalence relation}{}
  Let \((n, m)\), \((k, l)\), and \((p, q)\) be any ordered pairs of the naturals. The relation
  defined in \cref{df:integers_definition} then follows
  \begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \((n, m) \sim (n, m)\) (Reflexivity)
    \item \([(n, m) \sim (k, l)] \implies [(k, l) \sim (n, m)]\) (Symmetry)
    \item \(\ab\big[[(n, m) \sim (k, l)] \land [(k, l) \sim (p, q)]] \implies [(n, m) \sim (p, q)]\)
          (Transitivity)
  \end{enumerate}
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
  Since \(n + m = m + n\), \((n, m) \sim (n, m)\) and the relation is reflexive.

  Now assume that \((n, m) \sim (k, l)\), i.e. \(n + l = m + k\). It also follows that
  \(k + m = l + n\); hence, \((k, l) \sim (n, m)\) and the relation is symmetric.

  Now assume that both \((n, m) \sim (k, l)\) and \((k, l) \sim (p, q)\), i.e.,
  \begin{equation}
    n + l = m + k \mathand k + p = l + q.
  \end{equation}
  Combining these two equations together yields
  \begin{equation}
    n + l + k + p = m + k + l + q.
  \end{equation}
  By the cancellation law on addition of naturals,
  \begin{equation}
    n + p = m + q;
  \end{equation}
  i.e., \((n, m) \sim (p, q)\): the relation is transitive.
\end{proof}

\section{Arithmetic on the integers}

We represent an integer by an equivalence class. If
\begin{equation}
  (n, m) \in \Zint{a},
\end{equation}
we write
\begin{equation}
  \Zint{a} = [(n, m)].
\end{equation}

As said earlier, equality denotes something that is \emph{absolutely identical}: two things are
equal if they are \emph{exactly the same}. But what do we mean when we say \emph{exactly the same?}

This is actually more of a philosophy question than a mathematics one. It's way beyond the scope of
this book. But a convincing argument is that two objects is the same if it \emph{behaves} in the
same way under any functions or logical sentences. It's like the duck test, which I shall remind you
again:
\begin{displayquote}
  \emph{If it looks like a duck, swims like a duck, and quacks like a duck, then it probably is a
  duck.}
\end{displayquote}

In mathematics, two objects are the same if it has the same properties under any
functions or sentences \(\phi\). I.e.,
\begin{equation}
  (x = y) \implies (\phi(x) = \phi(y)).
\end{equation}
This property is called the \textbf{Liebniz' substitution axiom}, or \textbf{well-definedness}.

For the sake of illustration, let's assume for now that addition is well-defined under
addition. Then, define \(\phi(x) = x + a\) where \(x\) and \(a\) is an integer. Since
addition is well-defined, \(x = y\) implies \(\phi(x) = \phi(y)\) or just \(x + a = y + a\):
you can add \(a\) to both sides. It is a crucial property that we've left out in the
naturals, because in the naturals, we \emph{postulate} that they are well-defined by
the Peano's axiom. But the integers doesn't have that postulate. Hence, we shall prove them here.

\begin{df}{Addition and multiplication on the integers}{}
  Let \(\Zint{a} = [(n, m)]\) and \(\Zint{b} = [(k, l)]\) then define
  \begin{gather}
    \Zint{a} + \Zint{b} = [(n + k, m + l)],                            \\
    \Zint{a} \times \Zint{b} = \Zint{a}\Zint{b} = [(nk + ml, nl + mk)]
  \end{gather}
\end{df}

\begin{prop}{Addition and multiplication on the integers are well-defined}{}
  Let \(\Zint{a} = [(n, m)]\), \(\Zint{b} = [(n', m')]\), and \(\Zint{c} = [(p, q)]\). If
  \(\Zint{a} = \Zint{b}\), then
  \begin{equation}
    \Zint{a} + \Zint{c} = \Zint{b} + \Zint{c} \mathand \Zint{a}\Zint{c} = \Zint{b}\Zint{c},
  \end{equation}
  also
  \begin{equation}
    \Zint{c} + \Zint{a} = \Zint{c} + \Zint{b} \mathand \Zint{c}\Zint{a} = \Zint{c}\Zint{b}.
  \end{equation}
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
  For addition, consider
  \begin{equation}
    \Zint{a} + \Zint{c} = [(n + p, m + q)] \mathand \Zint{b} + \Zint{c} = [(n' + p, m' + q)].
  \end{equation}
  These two are equivalent \ifft \(n + p + m' + q = n' + p + m + q\). By \(\Zint{a} = \Zint{b}\),
  \((n, m) \sim (n', m')\) and
  \begin{equation}
    n + m' = n' + m.
  \end{equation}
  Adding \(p + q\) to each side yields:
  \begin{align}
    n + m' + p + q & = n' + m + p + q  \\
    n + p + m' + q & = n' + p + m + q;
  \end{align}
  Hence \(\Zint{a} + \Zint{c} = \Zint{b} + \Zint{c}\). The other statement on addition can be
  proven by the same method.

  For mutliplication, consider
  \begin{equation}
    \Zint{a}\Zint{c} = [(np + mq, nq + mp)] \mathand \Zint{b}\Zint{c} = [(n'p + m'q, n'q + m'p)].
  \end{equation}
  These two are equivalent if
  \begin{equation}
    np + mq + n'p + m'q = nq + mp + n'q + m'p.
  \end{equation}
  By \(\Zint{a} = \Zint{b}\), \(n + m' = n' + m\). Multiply through by \(p\) and \(q\)
  to get two equations:
  \begin{gather}
    np + m'p = n'p + mp, \\
    nq + m'q = n'q + mq.
  \end{gather}
  Flipping the first one and adding them together, we get
  \begin{align}
    np + n'p + mq + m'q & = mp + m'p + nq + n'q  \\
    np + mq + n'p + m'q & = nq + mp + n'q + m'p;
  \end{align}
  hence, \(\Zint{a}\Zint{c} \sim \Zint{b}\Zint{c}\).
\end{proof}

Since the integers are proven to be well-defined under addition and multiplication, we can safely
substitute the \(\sim\) symbol for a proper equality sign, \(=\).

Next, we investigate the trichotomy of integers by first defining the negation:

\begin{df}{Negation of integers}{}
  The negation of an integer \(\Zint{a} = (n, m)\) is the integer \((m, n)\), denoted as
  \(\Zint{-a}\)
\end{df}

\begin{lemma}{Trichotomy of the integers}{integers-trichotomy}
  Let \(\Zint{a} = (n, m)\) be an integer, then only one of these statements are true:
  \begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \(\Zint{a}\) is positive, i.e., \(\exists (k \in \mathbb{N}) : [(n, m)] \sim (k, 0)\).
    \item \(\Zint{a}\) is negative, i.e., \(\exists (k \in \mathbb{N}) : [(n, m)] \sim (0, k)\).
    \item \(\Zint{a}\) is zero, i.e., \([(n, m)] \sim (0, 0)\).
  \end{itemize}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
  First, we prove that the classes of type \([(k, 0)]\), \([(0, 0)]\) and \([(0, k)]\) are disjoint,
  then we use the trichotomy of order on the naturals to show that the integers must belong in at
  least one of these three families.

  Let \(p, q\) be natural numbers s.t. \(p \neq q\). Then, \(\neg((p, 0) \sim (q, 0))\). If it
  was, then \(p + 0 = 0 + q\), contradicting the assumption that \(p \neq q\). Similarly,
  \(\neg((0, p) ~ (q, 0))\) as well because if it was, then \(0 + q = 0 + p\), contradicting
  \(p \neq q\) aswell. By \cref{pr:integers-equivalence_class_disjoint}, these equivalence classes
  are all disjoint. Note that the zero class is also disjoint from the others as zero is also a
  natural number.

  By the trichotomy of order on the naturals (\cref{le:naturals_order_trichotomy}), there are then
  three cases:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item If \(n = m\), then \(n + 0 = 0 + m\) hence \((n, m) \sim (0, 0)\).
    \item If \(\exists (k \in \mathbb{N}) : n + k = m\), then \(n + k = m + 0\) hence
          \((n, m) \sim (0, k)\).
    \item If \(\exists (k \in \mathbb{N}) : n = m + k\), then \(n + 0 = m + k\) hence
          \((n, m) \sim (k, 0)\).
  \end{itemize}
  Hence, the integers are trichotomous.
\end{proof}

Due to this trichotomy, we shall use this notation from now on
\begin{df}{Representative numbers of the integers}{}
  Let \(\Zint{a}\) be an integer.
  \begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item If \(\Zint{a} = [(k, 0)]\), then write \(k\) in place of \(\Zint{a}\).
    \item If \(\Zint{a} = [(0, k)]\), then write \(-k\) in place of \(\Zint{a}\).
    \item If \(\Zint{a} = [(0, 0)]\), then write \(0\) in place of \(\Zint{a}\).
  \end{itemize}
\end{df}
The negative sign is justified due to the following corollary:
\begin{cor}{}{}
  Let \(\Zint{a} \in \mathbb{Z}\) and \(k \in \mathbb{N}\). If \(\Zint{a} = [(k, 0)]\), then
  \(-\Zint{a} = [(0, k)]\), and if \(\Zint{a} = [(0, k)]\) then \(-\Zint{a} = [(k, 0)]\). Also,
  \((-1) \times \Zint{a} = -\Zint{a}\).
\end{cor}

\paragraph{Embedding}

From the justification above, in a sense, the integers is capable of fully representing the naturals
even though the integers itself doesn't have the same mathematical structure as the naturals. As we
shall see later, the properties of arithmetic from the naturals also carried over to the integers.
In mathematics, we say that the naturals are \textbf{embedded} onto the integers. In a similar
manner, the integers can be embedded into the rationals, and the rationals can be embedded into the
reals.

For now, we'll keep the word embedding as a vague idea. A system is embedded in another larger
system if the larger system is fully capable of representing the smaller system. The term will be
made rigorous later in the subject of topology.

The basic laws of arithmetic on the integers is mostly the same as the naturals, but with the
addition of negative signs. The following proposition collects them all into one big box for
reference sake. However, the proof is more exhausting than anything because it requires us to expand
the integers into their ordered pairs representation, and it doesn't require some clever induction
arguments like on the naturals. So in some sense, it is chorework. Still, it is included for the
sake of completeness.

Since there is a lot going on, let's temporarily drop the dagger notation for ease of readability.

\begin{prop}{Arithmetic on the integers}{integers-arithmetic_rules}
  For all \(a, b, c \in \mathbb{Z}\)
  \begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \(a + b = b + a\) \label{ite:integers-arithmetic_rules_1}
    \item \((a + b) + c = a + (b + c)\) \label{ite:integers-arithmetic_rules_2}
    \item \(a + 0 = a\) \label{ite:integers-arithmetic_rules_3}
    \item \(a + (-a) = 0\) \label{ite:integers-arithmetic_rules_4}
    \item \(ab = ba\) \label{ite:integers-arithmetic_rules_5}
    \item \((ab)c = a(bc)\) \label{ite:integers-arithmetic_rules_6}
    \item \(1 \times a = a\) \label{ite:integers-arithmetic_rules_7}
    \item \(a(b + c) = ab + ac\) \label{ite:integers-arithmetic_rules_8}
    \item \((a + b)c = ac + bc\) \label{ite:integers-arithmetic_rules_9}
  \end{enumerate}
\end{prop}

For the sake of simplicity, when I state that an integer \(k = (n, m)\), I mean \(k\) is the
equivalence class of \([(n, m)]\).

Let \(a = (n, m)\), \(b = (k, l)\) and \(c = (p, q)\).

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{ite:integers-arithmetic_rules_1}]
  \begin{align}
    a + b & = (n, m) + (k, l)                  \\
          & = (n + k, m + l)                   \\
          & = (k + n, l + m)                   \\
          & = (k, l) + (n, m) = b + a \qedhere
  \end{align}
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{ite:integers-arithmetic_rules_2}]
  \begin{align}
    (a + b) + c & = \ab\big((n, m) + (k, l)) + (p, q)                           \\
                & = (n + k, m + l) + (p, q)                                     \\
                & = (n + k + p, m + l + q)                                      \\
                & = (n + (k + p), m + (l + q))                                  \\
                & = (n + m) + (k + p, l + q)                                    \\
                & = (n + m) + \ab\big((k + l) + (p + q)) = a + (b + c) \qedhere
  \end{align}
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{ite:integers-arithmetic_rules_3}]
  \begin{align}
    a + 0 & = (n, m) + (0, 0)      \\
          & = (n + 0, m + 0)       \\
          & = (n, m) = a. \qedhere
  \end{align}
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{ite:integers-arithmetic_rules_4}]
  \begin{align}
    a + (-a) & = (n, m) + (m, n)            \\
             & = (n + m, m + n)             \\
             & = (n + m, n + m) \sim (0, 0) \\
             & = 0 \qedhere
  \end{align}
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{ite:integers-arithmetic_rules_5}]
  \begin{align}
    ab & = (n, m) \times (k, l)                \\
       & = (nk + ml, nl + mk)                  \\
       & = (kn + lm, ln + km)                  \\
       & = (k, l) \times (n, m) = ba. \qedhere
  \end{align}
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{ite:integers-arithmetic_rules_6}]
  \begin{align}
    (ab)c & = \ab\big((n, m) \times (k, l)) \times (p, q)                   \\
          & = (nk + ml, nl + mk) \times (p, q)                              \\
          & = (nkp + mlp + nlq + mkq, nkq + mlq + nlp + mkp)                \\
          & = (nkl + nlq + mkq + mlp, nkq + nlp + mkp + mlq)                \\
          & = (n, m) \times (kp + lq, kq + lp)                              \\
          & = (n, m) \times \ab\big((k, l) \times (p, q)) = n(mk). \qedhere
  \end{align}
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{ite:integers-arithmetic_rules_7}]
  \begin{align}
    1 \times a & = (1, 0) \times (n, m) \\
               & = (1n + 0m, 1m + 0n)   \\
               & = (n, m) = a.
  \end{align}
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{ite:integers-arithmetic_rules_8}]
  \begin{align}
    a(b + c) & = (n, m) \times \ab\big((k, l) + (p, q))                                            \\
             & = (n, m) \times (k + p, l + q)                                                      \\
             & = (nk + np + ml + mq, nl + nq + mk + mp)                                            \\
             & = (nk + ml, nl + mk) + (np + mq, nq + mp)                                           \\
             & = \ab\big((n, m) \times (k, l)) + \ab\big((n, m) \times (p, q)) = ab + ac. \qedhere
  \end{align}
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{ite:integers-arithmetic_rules_9}]
  \begin{align}
    (a + b)c & = \ab\big((n, m) + (k, l)) \times (p, q)                                            \\
             & = (n + k, m + l) \times (p, q)                                                      \\
             & = (np + kp + mq + lq, nq + kq + mp + lp)                                            \\
             & = (np + mq, nq + mp) + (kp + lq, kq + lp)                                           \\
             & = \ab\big((n, m) \times (p, q)) + \ab\big((k, l) \times (p, q)) = ac + bc. \qedhere
  \end{align}
\end{proof}

Properties about multiplication with zero is carried over from the naturals. Here, we use the dagger
notation to separate the integers from the naturals for clarity.

\begin{prop}{}{}
  \begin{equation}
    \forall (\Zint{a}, \Zint{b} \in \mathbb{Z}) : \Zint{a}\Zint{b}
    = \Zint{0}
    \implies [(\Zint{a} = \Zint{0}) \lor (\Zint{b} = \Zint{0})]
  \end{equation}
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}
  If both \(\Zint{a}\) and \(\Zint{b}\) are positive, then \(\Zint{a} = (a, 0)\) and
  \(\Zint{b} = (b, 0)\). The statement \(\Zint{a}\Zint{b} = \Zint{0}\) then becomes
  \begin{equation}
    (a, 0) \times (b, 0) = (ab, 0) \sim (0, 0),
  \end{equation}
  i.e., \(ab = 0\). By \cref{co:naturals-product_is_zero_implies_some_is_zero}, either \(a = 0\) or
  \(b = 0\); hence, either \(\Zint{a} = \Zint{0}\) or \(\Zint{b} = \Zint{0}\) as well.

  Now assume that one of the integers are negative. Without loss of generality, we only need to
  assume that the first one is negative. If the second one is negative, we can always use the
  commutativity of multiplication to switch their places. Let \(\Zint{a} = (a, 0)\) and
  \(\Zint{b} = (0, b)\). Then,
  \begin{equation}
    (a, 0) \times (0, b) = (0, ab),
  \end{equation}
  i.e., \(ab = 0\). We've again reached the same conclusion.

  If both of them are negative, i.e., \(\Zint{a} = (0, a)\) and \(\Zint{b} = (0, b)\), then their
  product is \((ab, 0)\). And the same conclusion is also reached.
\end{proof}

\begin{cor}{Cancellation laws for the integers}{}
  \begin{equation}
    \forall (\Zint{a}, \Zint{b}, \Zint{c} \in \mathbb{Z}) : \ab\big[(\Zint{a}\Zint{c} = \Zint{b}\Zint{c}) \land (\Zint{c} \neq 0)] \implies [\Zint{a} = \Zint{b}]
  \end{equation}
\end{cor}

\begin{proof}
  If \(\Zint{a}\Zint{c} = \Zint{b}\Zint{c}\), then
  \begin{equation}
    \Zint{a}\Zint{c} - \Zint{b}\Zint{c} = (\Zint{a} - \Zint{b})\Zint{c} = \Zint{0}.
  \end{equation}
  By the preceding proposition, either \(\Zint{c}\) or \(\Zint{a} - \Zint{b}\) is zero. But since
  \(\Zint{c} \neq 0\), \(\Zint{a} - \Zint{b}\) must be zero. Adding \(\Zint{b}\) to both sides, we
  get
  \begin{align}
    \Zint{a} - \Zint{b} + \Zint{b} & = \Zint{0} + \Zint{b} \\
    \Zint{a}                       & = \Zint{b}. \qedhere
  \end{align}
\end{proof}

Here, all the laws of arithmetic on the integers has been established. Moving forward, we shall
completely drop the dagger notation.

\section{Ordering of the integers}

The orderings of the integers can be imposed in the same way it was imposed on the natural numbers.

\begin{df}{Orders on the integers}{}
  For all \(a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\), \(a\) is strictly greater than \(b\), and \(b\) is strictly less
  than \(a\), denoted \(a > b\) or \(b < a\) \ifft there exists a positive integer \(m\) such that
  \(a + m = b\).

  If \(m\) is allowed to also be zero, then \(a\) is greater than or equal to \(b\) and \(b\) is
  less than or equal to \(a\), denoted as \(a \geq b\) or \(b \leq a\).
\end{df}

The properties of orders on the integers is almost identical to the ones on the naturals, but with
negations introduced. Also similar to the order on the naturals, order on the integers is not
reflexive and symmetric, but it is transitive.

\begin{prop}{Properties of order on the integers}{}
  Let \(n, m, k \in \mathbb{Z}\), then
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \(n > m\) \ifft \(n - m\) is positive.
          \label{ite:integers-order_property_1}
    \item If \(n > m\), then \(n + k > m + k\). The same relation holds for the non-strict version
          of the relation.
          \label{ite:integers-order_property_2}
    \item If \(n > m\) and \(k\) is positive, then \(nk > mk\). But if \(k\) is negative,
          \(nk < mk\).\label{ite:integers-order_property_3}
  \end{enumerate}
\end{prop}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{ite:integers-order_property_1}]
  If \(n > m\), then there exists a positive integer \(a\) such that \(n = m + a\), then by adding
  \(-m\) onto both sides and using \cref{ite:integers-arithmetic_rules_4} of
  \cref{pr:integers-arithmetic_rules}, \(n - m = a\), a positive integer.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{ite:integers-order_property_2}]
  Assuming \(n = m + a\) as from earlier, adding \(k\) to both sides yields
  \((n + k) = (m + k) + a\). By the positivity of \(a\), \(n + k > m + k\). The same thing can be
  said for the non-strict version but replace every occurence of \enquote{positive} with
  \enquote{non-negative.}
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{ite:integers-order_property_3}]
  Assuming \(n = m + a\) as from earlier, multiplying both sides by a positive \(k\) yields
  \(nk = mk + ak\).

  If \(k\) is positive, it is represented by the equivalence class of \((k, 0)\). Since \(a\) is
  positive as well, it is represented by \((a, 0)\). Then,
  \begin{equation}
    (a, 0) \times (k, 0) = (ak + 0, 0 + 0) = (ak, 0):
  \end{equation}
  a positive integer. Hence, \(nk > mk\).

  But if \(k\) is negative, it is represented by \((0, k)\) and
  \begin{equation}
    (a, 0) \times (0, k) = (a0 + 0k, ak + 0) = (0, ak):
  \end{equation}
  a negative integer. Thus, \(-ak\) is positive. Adding \(-ak\) to both sides we get
  \begin{equation}
    mk = nk + (-ak):
  \end{equation}
  or just \(nk < mk\).
\end{proof}

From \cref{ite:integers-order_property_1} of the preceding proposition, we get a simple corollary:

\begin{cor}{Order trichotomy on the integers}{}
  Let \(n, m \in \mathbb{Z}\), then only one of these statements are true: \(n = m\), \(n > m\), or
  \(n < m\).
\end{cor}

\begin{proof}
  From \cref{le:integers-trichotomy}, \(n - m\) can be split into positive, negative,
  and zero. By the preceding proposition, we have the following conclusion:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item If \(n - m\) is positive, then \(n > m\).
    \item If \(n - m\) is negative, then \(m - n\) is positive hence \(m > n\) or \(n < m\).
    \item If \(n - m\) is zero, then \(n = m\), concluding the proof.
  \end{itemize}
\end{proof}
